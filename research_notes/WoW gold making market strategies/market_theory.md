## 1. Mean reversion and deal detection (moving averages, medians, z-scores, Bollinger bands, percentile rank, window choice)

### Takeaway
Robust statistics (median, MAD, trimmed/filtered means) are the right base for "is this cheap?" because auction data contain manipulated and outlier listings; mean/standard-deviation tools (z-score, Bollinger bands) are usable only on already-filtered series and their originator explicitly says band touches are not signals. Evidence that price *gaps* mean-revert exists for WoW across servers, but price *levels* in virtual economies drift (inflation, patches), so long-window "fair value" is unsafe.

### Cited Findings
- **Exponential moving average (definition, formula).** EWMA_t = λ·Y_t + (1−λ)·EWMA_{t−1}, 0 < λ ≤ 1; λ is usually set between 0.2 and 0.3 (attributed to Hunter 1986) though the choice is somewhat arbitrary; λ = 1 reduces to reacting only to the latest point; variance of the statistic ≈ [λ/(2−λ)]·s², control limits EWMA_0 ± k·s_ewma with k usually 3. — [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook §6.3.2.4](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc324.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **Simple exponential smoothing (same recursion, textbook form).** Level ℓ_t = α·y_t + (1−α)·ℓ_{t−1}; weights on past observations decay geometrically (α = 0.2: 0.2, 0.16, 0.128, …); small α = smoother, large α = follows recent data; appropriate only for data with no clear trend or seasonal pattern. — [Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, *Forecasting: Principles and Practice* 3rd ed., §8.1](https://otexts.com/fpp3/ses.html) [textbook]
- **Bollinger bands (originator's rules).** Defaults: 20 periods for the average and standard deviation, 2 standard deviations for width, described as starting points. "Tags of the bands are just that, tags not signals" (Rule 6); closes outside the bands are initially continuation signals, not reversals (Rule 8); prices can "walk" a band in a trend (Rule 7). Scale the multiplier with the window: 2.0 at 20 periods, 2.1 at 50, 1.9 at 10 (Rule 11). Traditional bands use a simple moving average because the standard deviation uses one (Rule 12). %b locates price within the bands; BandWidth = (upper − lower) / middle, which with default parameters equals four times the coefficient of variation. No statistical/normality assumption is made; about 90% of data falls inside with defaults. — [John Bollinger, "Bollinger Band Rules"](https://www.bollingerbands.com/bollinger-band-rules) [reference, originator's own site]
- **Z-score and its small-sample ceiling.** Z_i = (Y_i − Ȳ)/s. The maximum possible Z-score is (n−1)/√n, so Z-scores can be misleading, particularly for small samples. — [NIST e-Handbook §1.3.5.17](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35h.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **Modified (robust) z-score.** M_i = 0.6745·(x_i − median)/MAD, recommended by Iglewicz and Hoaglin; |M_i| > 3.5 labels a potential outlier. — [NIST e-Handbook §1.3.5.17](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35h.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **Why median-based scale beats mean-based.** MAD = median(|Y_i − median(Y)|). The standard deviation can be greatly distorted by tail behaviour; MAD is less affected by tail values than the average absolute deviation; the interquartile range ignores tails entirely; the range depends only on the two most extreme points and is not robust. MAD and IQR trade robustness for some efficiency when data really are normal. — [NIST e-Handbook §1.3.5.6 Measures of Scale](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **A peer-reviewed paper is titled exactly as the recommendation**: Leys, Ley, Klein, Bernard & Licata (2013), "Detecting outliers: Do not use standard deviation around the mean, use absolute deviation around the median", *J. Experimental Social Psychology* 49(4):764–766. Only the bibliographic record was readable (no abstract); the title is the only content verified. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.013) [peer-reviewed; metadata only]
- **Industry precedent for robust "market value" on WoW auction data (TradeSkillMaster).** Per scan: sort buyouts ascending; after passing 15% of auctions, a price jump ≥ 20% between consecutive auctions discards that auction and everything above; consider at most the lowest 30%; then drop points more than 1.5 standard deviations from the mean of the survivors; average the rest. Scans within a day are averaged to a daily value; the displayed value is a weighted average of the previous 14 days plus today with a half-life of "a little over 2 days". Worked example: a plain average of the 24 sample prices gives 25.79 ("obviously too high") versus 14.5 from the algorithm. The filter exists to stop high-volume listings at inflated prices from distorting the value. — [TSM documentation, "AuctionDB Market Value"](https://support.tradeskillmaster.com/tsm-addon-documentation/auctiondb-market-value) [community, tool documentation]
- **TSM price-source windows.** DBMinBuyout = lowest auction at last update; DBRecent = market-value algorithm on the latest snapshot only; DBMarket = 14-day weighted average favouring the most recent 3 days; DBHistorical = 60-day average of DBMarket; region variants average across realms. — [TSM documentation, "Which Price Sources can I use"](https://support.tradeskillmaster.com/custom-strings/which-price-sources-can-i-use-and-what-do-they-mean) [community, tool documentation]
- **Mean reversion evidence in equities is horizon-dependent.** Poterba & Summers (NBER WP 2343; *J. Financial Economics* 22:27–59, 1988): US returns 1871–1986, 17 other countries 1957–85; "stock returns are positively serially correlated over short horizons" and negatively over long horizons; transitory components account for a large share of return variance; variance-ratio tests have low power. — [NBER w2343](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2343) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Counter-evidence / caution.** Lo & MacKinlay (NBER WP 2168; *Review of Financial Studies* 1(1):41–66, 1988): weekly returns 1962–1985; "the random walk model is strongly rejected", but the rejection does *not* support a mean-reverting stationary model. — [NBER w2168](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2168) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Mean reversion of price *differentials* in WoW (peer-reviewed).** Morrison & Fontenla (2017), "Purchasing power parity across eight worlds", *Economics Letters* 158:62–66: price data from eight replica WoW servers including the Cataclysm expansion shock; price levels stay within an estimated "band of inaction" created by trade barriers and are highly correlated; when they diverge, arbitrage opportunities appear and differences quickly revert to the mean. — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecolet:v:158:y:2017:i:c:p:62-66) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Virtual-goods price levels are tested for unit roots (non-stationarity) rather than assumed mean-reverting.** Scholten, Cowling, Hawick & Walker (2019) apply log transforms, first differencing and Augmented Dickey–Fuller tests to 180 days of daily Old School RuneScape prices for 3,467 items (3,358 after removing static series), and use the coefficient of variation c_v = σ/μ as the per-item volatility measure. (The summary of the paper's ADF table and its text were flagged as mutually inconsistent when I read it, so do not quote the unit-root verdicts.) — [arXiv:1905.06721](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06721) [working paper; IEEE copyright notice but venue not named on the page]
- **Stability of WoW prices over a month (peer-reviewed, small sample).** Kosminsky (2010), *J. Virtual Worlds Research* 2(4): netherweave cloth on six servers, 13 Sep–13 Oct 2008, ≥ 1,000 auctions per server from wowecon.com; 30-day median vs mean were nearly identical on each server (e.g. 12.00 vs 12.29; 17.50 vs 17.48) with standard deviations of 0.87–2.61 on prices of ~12–17.5. — [JVWR article PDF](https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/869/634) [peer-reviewed; full text read]

### Inferences
- **Fair-value estimate from the four stored fields.** The addon stores only the *lowest* price per hour, which is the single statistic most exposed to bait/misprice listings. Use `rollingMedian(minPrice, W)` as fair value and `1.4826·MAD` (equivalently MAD/0.6745) as the scale; deal score = `0.6745·(minPrice_now − median_W)/MAD_W`; flag a deal when the score < −3.5 (NIST threshold) *and* the item has enough observations. If the full listing ladder is available at scan time, compute a TSM-style filtered mean then and store it as a fifth field; it cannot be reconstructed afterwards from min/max/quantity/count.
- **Percentile rank** of the current price in its own history: `rank = (# of stored minPrice values < current) / N`. No distributional assumption; a "bottom 10%" rule is the non-parametric equivalent of a lower Bollinger tag. (Standard statistic; I did not open a dedicated source for it.)
- **Bollinger on auction data.** Use `median ± k·1.4826·MAD` rather than `SMA ± k·SD`, or apply SMA/SD only to a filtered series. Use %b as the normalized "how cheap" value and BandWidth as a volatility/squeeze indicator. Per Bollinger's own rules, a lower-band touch is a setup, not a buy signal: require confirmation (e.g. quantity not exploding, see section 8).
- **Window choice.** Candidates grounded in the sources: ~3 days (TSM's effective weighting, half-life ~2 days) for fast-moving commodities; 14 days (TSM DBMarket) as the default; 60 days (DBHistorical) as the slow anchor. Because hourly sampling in an addon is irregular (only when the player scans), define windows in *days of calendar time*, aggregate to one robust value per day first (TSM does this), then smooth across days. EMA with α ≈ 0.2–0.3 on the daily series matches the NIST convention.
- **Decision supported.** Buy when price is far below a robust centre and the centre itself is stable; sell/relist target = the robust centre, not the historical maximum.
- **Failure modes.** (1) Level shifts (patch, expansion, inflation) make "below the 60-day median" a falling knife; pair with regime detection (section 2). (2) Z-scores with few observations are capped at (n−1)/√n, so with n = 10 a score can never exceed 2.85; require a minimum n. (3) MAD = 0 for items whose price barely moves; fall back to a percentage threshold. (4) Minimum price is set by one seller; a single 1-unit bait listing looks like a "deal" but cannot be bought in size (section 4). (5) Evidence for mean reversion is strong for *spreads between linked markets* (Morrison & Fontenla) and mixed for raw price levels (Lo & MacKinlay).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests mean reversion of single-item WoW auction prices over time on one realm.
- Leys et al. (2013) full text and abstract were not readable (ScienceDirect 403); only the title is verified.
- I did not open Bollinger's book (*Bollinger on Bollinger Bands*, 2001); the rules page on his site was used instead.
- Percentile rank and trimmed-mean definitions were not sourced to an opened reference.

## 2. Trend and momentum; regime changes versus noise

### Takeaway
Rate of change and moving-average crossovers are simple to compute and have peer-reviewed support in equities, but are whipsaw-prone; for "a patch moved the price level" the appropriate tools are change-detection charts (CUSUM, EWMA), which are designed to catch small sustained mean shifts that a single-point threshold misses.

### Cited Findings
- **Rate of change.** ROC = [(Close − Close n periods ago) / (Close n periods ago)] × 100; default 12 periods; centre-line crossovers are "prone to whipsaw, especially short-term"; divergences "fail to foreshadow reversals more often than not"; oversold readings can persist while a decline continues. — [StockCharts ChartSchool, "Rate of Change (ROC)"](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-indicators/rate-of-change-roc) [reference] (claim rests on a reference site only)
- **Moving-average and trading-range-break rules have peer-reviewed empirical support.** Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992), *J. Finance* 47(5):1731–1764: Dow Jones Index 1897–1986, bootstrap tests; "strong support for the technical strategies"; returns after buy signals are higher and less volatile than after sell signals; results inconsistent with random walk, AR(1), GARCH-M and EGARCH nulls. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v47y1992i5p1731-64.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Short-horizon momentum, long-horizon reversal.** Positive serial correlation over short horizons and negative over long horizons in stock returns. — [Poterba & Summers, NBER w2343](https://www.nber.org/papers/w2343) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **CUSUM for mean-shift detection.** S_hi(i) = max(0, S_hi(i−1) + x_i − μ̂_0 − k); S_lo(i) = max(0, S_lo(i−1) + μ̂_0 − k − x_i); start at 0; signal when either exceeds h. Choose k as half the shift to detect (k = δ·σ/2); rule-of-thumb h ≈ 4–5 (in σ units). CUSUM charts are "more efficient in detecting small shifts in the mean" than Shewhart (single-point) charts, specifically for shifts of 2σ or less. — [NIST e-Handbook §6.3.2.3](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc323.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **EWMA chart for drift.** Because the EWMA averages all prior data, a suitable λ makes it sensitive to small or gradual drift, whereas a Shewhart chart reacts only when the latest point crosses a limit. — [NIST e-Handbook §6.3.2.4](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc324.htm) [reference, official handbook]
- **Moving-average trend estimates over-smooth sudden moves.** Classical decomposition's trend-cycle tends to over-smooth rapid rises and falls and has no estimate for the first and last few observations. — [Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, FPP3 §3.4](https://otexts.com/fpp3/classical-decomposition.html) [textbook]
- **Game updates are real regime shocks in WoW data.** Morrison & Fontenla (2017) treat the Cataclysm expansion as a major shock in their eight-server WoW price data. — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecolet:v:158:y:2017:i:c:p:62-66) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Developer interventions shift price levels measurably.** In Old School RuneScape, an item sink (developer buys and deletes items) raised prices of targeted items by about 7% (95% CI 5.0–9.6%) in round 1 and 14.4% (11.9–16.9%) in round 2 (difference-in-differences; daily data for ~4,000 items, Aug 2021–Aug 2022), with no significant change in trade volume. — [Hogan-Hennessy, Xenopoulos & Silva (2022), arXiv:2210.07970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper; numbers from the full text via ar5iv]
- **Supply-side "technology" changes cause sustained deflation.** EVE Online Q1 2010: Primary Producer Price Index −14.2% in the quarter (−8% in February alone), Tech II construction components −22%, attributed to changed blueprint requirements and scannable asteroid belts introduced in the Dominion expansion ("more efficient production"). — [CCP, *Quarterly Economic Newsletter* Q1 2010, ed. Dr. Eyjólfur Guðmundsson](https://cdn1.eveonline.com/community/QEN/QEN_Q1-2010.pdf) [official]

### Inferences
- **Signals.** `ROC_n = minPrice_daily(t)/minPrice_daily(t−n) − 1` on the daily robust series (n = 1, 3, 7 days). Crossover: fast EMA (≈3 d) versus slow EMA (≈14 d); state = up/down. Both computable from stored snapshots after daily aggregation.
- **Regime detector.** Standardize the daily log price against a baseline window: z_t = (ln p_t − median_base)/ (1.4826·MAD_base). Run two-sided CUSUM with k = 0.5 and h = 4–5. On alarm: (a) mark a regime break at the time the CUSUM last left zero, (b) discard history before the break for fair-value estimation (restart the window), (c) suppress "deal" flags until the new window has enough points. This directly addresses "patch shifts the level vs noise": one outlier hour cannot trip CUSUM, a sustained 1σ shift does.
- **Decision supported.** Do not buy "cheap vs history" items in a confirmed down-regime; do not sell at old fair value in a confirmed up-regime; widen/shorten windows after a break.
- **Failure modes.** Whipsaw in range-bound items (cited above); crossovers lag by construction; CUSUM needs a reasonable σ estimate, and an outlier-inflated σ hides real shifts (use MAD); irregular sampling creates fake "jumps" if a gap in scans spans a real gradual move; the equity evidence (Brock et al.) is on a liquid index, not on thin single-seller markets.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that evaluates momentum or crossover rules on MMO auction data.
- A 2011 NYCU master's thesis (Chen, "Price Data Analysis in Online Game Worlds") reportedly finds WoW prices rise as servers age and react immediately to major game changes, but the link returned 404 and it could not be verified; treat as unconfirmed.
- Structural-break econometrics (e.g. Bai–Perron) were not researched; CUSUM/EWMA were chosen as the simplest implementable option.

## 3. Seasonality: hour-of-day and day-of-week patterns from short histories

### Takeaway
Classical decomposition (moving-average trend, then per-season averages) is the simplest textbook method and is feasible in an addon, but it assumes a stable repeating pattern, loses the ends of the series and is not robust to outliers. Published evidence for weekly cycles in WoW auction data is community-grade only and reports clearer cycles in *quantity* than in *price*.

### Cited Findings
- **Classical additive decomposition (algorithm).** (1) Estimate trend-cycle with a 2×m moving average if the seasonal period m is even, or an m-MA if odd; (2) detrend by subtraction; (3) average the detrended values for each season and adjust them to sum to zero; (4) remainder = data − trend − seasonal. **Multiplicative:** divide instead of subtract; seasonal indices are adjusted to sum to m. — [Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, FPP3 §3.4](https://otexts.com/fpp3/classical-decomposition.html) [textbook]
- **Stated limitations.** No trend (hence no remainder) estimate for the first and last few observations; over-smooths rapid rises and falls; assumes the seasonal component repeats unchanged; not robust to occasional unusual values. — [FPP3 §3.4](https://otexts.com/fpp3/classical-decomposition.html) [textbook]
- **WoW weekly pattern (community evidence, 2012).** A WoW Insider "Gold Capped" column using auction graphs for the Proudmoore server reports: supply is highest as servers reset on Tuesday and then falls sharply over the following days; auction quantities spike on weekends, which the author reads as the market restocking; prices are much less predictable than quantities, e.g. Volcanic Potion price "jumped nearly 50%" in one low-supply week while Inferno Ruby quantity spikes had no appreciable price effect; the author concludes "there's no perfect pattern". No data source or date range is given. — [Engadget / WoW Insider, Fox Van Allen, 7 Feb 2012](https://www.engadget.com/2012/02/07/what-days-should-you-buy-or-sell-on-the-auction-house/) [community] (claim rests on a community source only)
- **Hourly snapshots have been used academically for WoW seller analysis.** Hsu, Togelius & Sun collected the WoW auction list hourly for two months across eight Taiwanese faction markets and derived each seller's "success transactions" by comparing successive listings. (Seasonality was not analysed.) — [Hsu, Togelius & Sun, "Towards market seller modeling in World of Warcraft", IEEE 2014 short paper](https://www.cse.unr.edu/~sushil/pubs/newestPapers/aux/paper_104.pdf) [peer-reviewed conference short paper; full text read]
- **Daily granularity hides intraday structure in published datasets.** Jagex's official Grand Exchange prices are delayed by up to 24 hours, which is why researchers used the OSRS Wiki/RuneLite feed for daily averages. — [Hogan-Hennessy et al. (2022), arXiv:2210.07970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper]; the wiki feed itself exposes 5-minute and 1-hour averages with traded counts — [OSRS Wiki, Real-time Prices API](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/RuneScape:Real-time_Prices) [community, primary data]

### Inferences
- **Simple seasonal indices for an addon.** Work in ratios (multiplicative) so cheap and expensive items are comparable: `ratio_t = minPrice_t / trend_t` where `trend_t` = centred 7-day rolling median of the daily value. Day-of-week index = median of `ratio` over all observations on that weekday; hour-of-day index = median of `minPrice_t / dailyMedian(day of t)` per hour bucket. Normalize indices to average 1. Do the same for `totalQty` (the community evidence says quantity cycles are the stronger effect).
- **Minimum history.** A weekly index needs several complete weeks: the 7-day centred trend loses 3 days at each end, and with fewer than about 3–4 observations per weekday the median is not meaningful. Hour-of-day indices need many scans in each hour bucket; because an addon scans only when the player is at the auction house, most buckets will be empty or biased toward the player's play hours. Report an index only when its bucket has ≥ n observations, otherwise show "insufficient data".
- **Significance check without statistics machinery.** Treat a weekday effect as real only if the weekday medians' spread (max index − min index) exceeds the typical within-weekday MAD.
- **Decision supported.** Buy on the weekday/hour with the lowest index and post on the highest; schedule restocking of consumables before the weekly demand peak.
- **Failure modes.** Patches and events break the "seasonality repeats" assumption; a regime break (section 2) inside the window contaminates the indices; survivorship of scan times; a pooled index across items (to gain sample size) assumes items share a cycle, which holds better within a category (raid consumables) than across categories.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or working-paper study of hour-of-day or day-of-week effects in WoW or other MMO auction data was found. The only evidence located is the 2012 community column above.
- French (1980), "Stock returns and the weekend effect", *J. Financial Economics* 8(1):55–69, is the classic equity day-of-week paper, but only its bibliographic record could be opened (no abstract), so no finding is attributed to it.
- STL and other robust decompositions were not researched; FPP3 notes classical decomposition is not robust to unusual values.

## 4. Liquidity and volume (turnover, days of supply, spread analogues, depth and walls, market impact, slippage)

### Takeaway
Liquidity signals need sales volume, which hourly listing snapshots do not contain; it must be estimated from quantity changes (with academic precedent) or from the player's own ledger, and tools in other games that show reliable volume do so because their games publish trade counts. Market impact grows with order size relative to daily volume (square-root law in financial markets); in an auction house it can be computed exactly only from the full listing ladder at scan time, not from the four stored fields.

### Cited Findings
- **Inventory turnover and days of supply (accounting definitions).** Inventory turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory; number of days' sales in inventory = average inventory ÷ average daily cost of goods sold; a rising days figure suggests slow sales or obsolescence. — [OpenStax, *Principles of Accounting Vol. 1* §10.5 (Franklin, Graybeal & Cooper, 2019)](https://openstax.org/books/principles-financial-accounting/pages/10-5-examine-the-efficiency-of-inventory-management-using-financial-ratios) [textbook]
- **Market value depends on how many units you need (walking the book).** In TSM's worked example, buying the 5 cheapest units costs 12.2 per item while buying the 15 cheapest costs 16.3 per item; TSM calls this "an inherent weakness with most market value estimates" and assumes the quantity needed is proportional to the number currently listed. — [TSM documentation, "AuctionDB Market Value"](https://support.tradeskillmaster.com/tsm-addon-documentation/auctiondb-market-value) [community, tool documentation]
- **Square-root market impact (empirical law).** Δ(Q) = Y·σ·√(Q/V): Δ = average relative price change over a "metaorder" of total size Q, σ = daily volatility, V = daily traded volume, Y a constant "of order unity". Data: nearly 500,000 proprietary trades on futures, June 2007–Dec 2010; holds for Q/V from a few 10⁻⁴ to a few percent; fitted exponents ≈ 0.5 (small-tick) to 0.6 (large-tick). The latent supply/demand profile is V-shaped and vanishes near the current price, so small orders have disproportionate impact. — [Tóth, Lempérière, Deremble, de Lataillade, Kockelkoren & Bouchaud (2011), *Physical Review X* 1, 021006; arXiv:1105.1694](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694) [peer-reviewed; full text read via ar5iv]
- **Linear impact / depth (theory).** Kyle (1985), *Econometrica* 53(6):1315–1335: with one insider, noise traders and competitive market makers, in the continuous-trading limit "the depth of the market is constant over time" and noise trading camouflages the insider. — [Econometric Society record](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1985/11/01/continuous-auctions-and-insider-trading) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]. Tóth et al. state that Kyle's model predicts impact linear in Q and does not account for the square-root behaviour. — [arXiv:1105.1694](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694) [peer-reviewed]
- **Impact is neither linear nor permanent (review).** — [Bouchaud (2009), "Price Impact", entry for the *Encyclopedia of Quantitative Finance*, arXiv:0903.2428](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.2428) [working paper; abstract only]
- **Spread as the source of flipping profit (two-sided markets).** Flipping "exploits the spread between pending buy orders and pending sell orders"; the margin is found by a test buy above market and test sell below market (e.g. bought at 135, sold at 130 ⇒ market between 130 and 135); flipping is "safer when the item has a high trade volume". — [OSRS Wiki, "Flipping"/Merchanting](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Flipping) [community]
- **Volume fields exposed by other games' tools.** OSRS real-time API: `/latest` returns `high`, `highTime`, `low`, `lowTime` per item (instant-buy and instant-sell prices); `/5m` and `/1h` return averaged high and low prices plus the number traded; `/mapping` includes the Grand Exchange buy `limit`. — [OSRS Wiki, Real-time Prices](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/RuneScape:Real-time_Prices) [community, primary data]. GE Tracker shows hourly Buying Quantity and Selling Quantity and a Buy/Sell Ratio ("x bought per hour / x sold per hour", ideally close to 1). — [GE Tracker, flipping basics](https://www.ge-tracker.com/landing/osrs-flipping) [community]. gw2tp screens "movers" only above liquidity thresholds (sell-price movers: last 24 h, demand > 10,000, supply > 4,000; flip preset: sorted by spread with buy ≥ 50, supply ≥ 500, demand ≥ 500). — [gw2tp.com](https://www.gw2tp.com/) [community]
- **Sales can be inferred from successive hourly snapshots (academic precedent).** "The success transactions are determined by comparing the successive items in the total selling list" (hourly WoW auction lists, 29 June–28 August, eight faction markets); over 90% of items on the shelf were trade goods. The paper also classifies sellers by normalized average supply per transaction into retailers (1–10), wholesalers (11–40) and distributors (40–250). — [Hsu, Togelius & Sun, IEEE 2014](https://www.cse.unr.edu/~sushil/pubs/newestPapers/aux/paper_104.pdf) [peer-reviewed conference short paper; full text read]
- **Trade is concentrated in few items.** In OSRS the top 20 items account for 69.7% of the top-100 items' traded volume over six months. — [Scholten et al. (2019), arXiv:1905.06721](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06721) [working paper]
- **Thin items are unreliable.** Low-volume items are easily moved by single traders, and items traded so rarely that their value is unknown are marked with question marks; beginners are told to avoid them. — [GE Tracker, flipping basics](https://www.ge-tracker.com/landing/osrs-flipping) [community]

### Inferences
- **Estimated sales (the missing input).** From snapshots: `soldEst(t) = max(0, totalQty(t−1) − totalQty(t))` summed over a day gives an *upper-bound-biased* proxy: decreases also come from cancellations and expiries, and same-hour restocking hides sales. Better: if the addon keeps the listing ladder between consecutive scans, count only units that vanished *from the cheap end* (bought units are the cheapest; expiries are spread through the ladder). Calibrate with the ledger: the player's own sales per day ÷ their share of listed quantity gives an independent turnover estimate.
- **Turnover and days of supply.** `turnover = soldEstPerDay / avg(totalQty)`; `daysOfSupply = totalQty_now / soldEstPerDay` (the accounting ratio with units instead of cost). Decision: prefer items with low days of supply for flipping (fast exit); a rising days-of-supply trend is an early glut warning.
- **Spread analogue in a sell-side-only book.** There are no bids, so use: (a) `discount = 1 − minPrice / robustFairValue` (how far the best ask sits below fair value, i.e. the buyer's edge); (b) `(maxPrice − minPrice)/minPrice` is computable from stored fields but is dominated by absurd high listings, so it is a poor dispersion measure; (c) round-trip cost = auction cut + expected lost deposits (section 5) plays the role of the half-spread a flipper must overcome.
- **Depth, walls, cost-to-clear, slippage.** With the full ladder at scan time: `costToClear(P) = Σ price_i·qty_i for price_i ≤ P`; `avgFillPrice(N)` = cost of the cheapest N units ÷ N; `slippage(N) = avgFillPrice(N)/minPrice − 1`; a wall = a price level holding more than x% of total quantity or more than y days of estimated sales. From the four stored fields these **cannot be computed**; only crude proxies exist: `avgStack = totalQty/numListings`, and a wall hint when `totalQty` is large while `minPrice` is static across many snapshots. Recommendation: at scan time derive and store a few ladder summaries (e.g. quantity within +5% and +20% of min, price of the N-th cheapest unit).
- **Impact rule of thumb.** If no ladder is available, the square-root law gives an order-of-magnitude estimate: expected price move ≈ σ_daily·√(N / soldEstPerDay), with σ_daily from the daily log-price MAD. Its validated range is small Q/V (up to a few percent) in continuous two-sided markets, so for N comparable to a day's volume treat it as a lower bound and rely on the ladder.
- **Failure modes.** Sales proxy inflated by cancel/relist wars (section 8) and expiry waves 12/24/48 h after mass postings; snapshots missing whenever the player is offline; total quantity includes overpriced stock that will never sell (compute supply only within a band above min when the ladder is known).

### Gaps

- TSM's region sale-rate and sold-per-day sources (how TSM estimates sale rates from its users' accounting data) were not defined on the documentation page I could open.
- No study measuring price impact or slippage in an MMO auction house was found.
- gw2bltc and GW2Efficiency pages did not return their data fields to the fetch tool; the gw2tp home page was the only GW2 tool verified.

## 5. Market-making and flipping economics (expected profit, probability of sale, inventory risk, holding time, opportunity cost, ROI per day)

### Takeaway
Net flip profit is sell price after the house cut, minus buy price, minus expected forfeited deposits; what dominates outcomes is the probability and time of sale, which finance models tie steeply to how far the ask sits from the best price and which must be calibrated from real executions (the ledger), not from hypothetical "price touched my level" logic. Community guides across games agree that volume and profit per unit time matter more than margin per unit.

### Cited Findings
- **WoW auction fees (retail description; page is tagged outdated and does not cover Classic variants).** "The house will take 5% of the winning bid as its cut"; Amount Received = Winning Bid × 0.95 + Deposit. Deposit depends on vendor sell price (VSP) and duration: 15% of VSP for 12 h, 30% for 24 h, 60% for 48 h, minimum 1 silver. If the item fails to sell, it is mailed back and "your deposit will not be refunded"; cancelling also forfeits the deposit. — [Warcraft Wiki, "Auction House"](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Auction_House) [community] (claim rests on a community source only; verify cut and deposit against the Forever client)
- **OSRS.** Most Grand Exchange sales pay a 2% tax capped at 5 million coins per item (1% from 9 Dec 2021, 2% from 29 May 2025); the tax rounds down so items under 50 coins pay none; buying is limited per item per 4 hours, selling is not; trades execute at the price of the earlier-placed offer. — [OSRS Wiki, "Grand Exchange"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Grand_Exchange) [community]
- **EVE Online.** Sales tax base 7.5% (about 3.37% with maximum skills); broker's fee base 3% at NPC stations, paid when an order is created and not refunded on cancel/expiry; modifying an order incurs a relist fee; "Station trading is all about volume." Margins are thin; the page advises trading many items to reduce single-item crash risk. — [EVE University Wiki, "Trading"](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Trading) [community]
- **Guild Wars 2.** Sellers pay two fees, a listing fee and an exchange fee; the redesigned UI shows both explicitly because the earlier "projected profit" omitted the exchange fee and confused players; the Trading Post works "more like a stock market than an auction house". (No percentages on this page.) — [ArenaNet, "Introducing the New Trading Post"](https://www.guildwars2.com/en/news/introducing-the-new-trading-post/) [official]
- **Margin and "profit per hour".** GE Tracker defines margin as "the price difference between the highest sell offer and the lowest buy offer"; the OSRS Wiki says "Quantity is usually more important than price" and "The key is profit per hour", recommends items of about 250–500 coins for bulk flipping, a test buy/resell "to see how quickly the item moves", never buying a huge quantity of an untested item, and holding "four to six good items". — [GE Tracker](https://www.ge-tracker.com/landing/osrs-flipping) [community]; [OSRS Wiki, "Flipping"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Flipping) [community]
- **Inventory risk shifts the price you should accept (market-making theory).** Avellaneda & Stoikov: a dealer holding q units with risk aversion γ and price volatility σ has reservation (indifference) price r(s,q,t) = s − q·γ·σ²·(T−t): "If the agent is long stock (q > 0), the reservation price is below the mid-price, indicating a desire to liquidate the inventory". Order arrivals are modelled as λ(δ) = A·exp(−k·δ), i.e. the fill rate decays exponentially in the distance δ of the quote from the mid-price; the optimal total spread is δᵃ + δᵇ = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k). In simulation the inventory-aware strategy gave P&L and final inventories with significantly less variance than a symmetric strategy. — [Avellaneda & Stoikov, "High-frequency trading in a limit order book", preprint dated 5 Oct 2006 (later published in *Quantitative Finance*, 2008; journal page not opened)](https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/LimitOrderBook.pdf) [working paper version of a peer-reviewed article; full text read]
- **Antecedent.** Avellaneda & Stoikov describe Ho & Stoll (1981, *J. Financial Economics* 9(1):47–73) as analysing optimal prices for a monopolistic dealer who sets bid and ask around a "true" price to account for inventory. — [same PDF](https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/LimitOrderBook.pdf) [working paper]; [Ho & Stoll bibliographic record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v9y1981i1p47-73.html) [peer-reviewed; metadata only]
- **Time-to-sale is steeply price-sensitive and must be estimated from real executions.** Lo, MacKinlay & Zhang (2002), *J. Financial Economics* 65:31–71: survival analysis of actual limit-order data (time-to-first-fill and time-to-completion); execution times are "very sensitive to limit price" but not to order size; hypothetical executions built from first-passage times or from transaction data are "very poor proxies for actual limit-order executions". — [Author's page (Andrew Lo, MIT)](https://web.mit.edu/~alo/www/Papers/limit10.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Undercut-and-reset dynamics (theory and evidence).** Maskin & Tirole (1988), *Econometrica* 56(3):571–599: in an alternating-move price-setting duopoly, Markov-perfect equilibria of the Edgeworth-cycle and kinked-demand types exist and profits stay above the competitive (Bertrand) level. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v56y1988i3p571-99.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]. Empirically the pattern is a sharp price spike followed by a run of small declines (daily station-level gasoline prices, 115 US cities); markets at both the least and most concentrated extremes are much less likely to cycle. — [Doyle, Muehlegger & Samphantharak (2010), *Energy Economics* 32(3):651–660](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/edgeworth-cycles-revisited-0) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Why sellers must undercut in WoW.** "In order to make a sale, a seller must be offering a good for a price less than or equal to every other price on the market at the time the sale is made." — [Kosminsky (2010), JVWR 2(4)](https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/869/634) [peer-reviewed; full text read]
- **TSM ledger-based cost basis.** `avgBuy`, `avgSell`, `maxBuy`/`minBuy`, and `SmartAvgBuy` = average purchase price over the last X purchases where X is the quantity currently held. — [TSM documentation, price sources](https://support.tradeskillmaster.com/custom-strings/which-price-sources-can-i-use-and-what-do-they-mean) [community, tool documentation]

### Inferences
- **Expected profit per unit (derived).** Let B = buy price, S = intended sell price, c = house cut (0.05 in the cited retail description), d = deposit per posting (refunded on sale, forfeited on expiry/cancel), p = probability a posting sells before it expires. Expected forfeited postings before a sale = (1−p)/p, so `E[profit | eventually sells] = S·(1−c) − B − d·(1−p)/p`. Break-even: `S_min = (B + d·(1−p)/p)/(1−c)`; with d ≈ 0 this is `B/0.95 ≈ 1.0526·B`. With a give-up rule after K postings and fallback value F (vendor/disenchant/dump price): `E[profit] = (1−(1−p)^K)·S(1−c) + (1−p)^K·F − B − d·E[failed postings]`.
- **Probability of sale p.** Estimate from the ledger: `p = sales / (sales + expiries + cancels)` per item, optionally bucketed by the posting's premium over the then-lowest price. An Avellaneda–Stoikov-style curve `p(δ) ∝ exp(−k·δ)` with δ = (myPrice − minPrice)/minPrice can be fitted per item class from those buckets. Without ledger history, a prior from snapshots: `min(1, soldEstPerDay·duration / quantityPricedAtOrBelowMine)`; per Lo–MacKinlay–Zhang such hypothetical fills are poor proxies, so label it low-confidence.
- **Holding time and return on capital.** `holdDays ≈ postingDuration/p` (geometric waiting) or the ledger's median (sale time − buy time). `ROI = E[profit]/B`; `ROI/day = ROI/holdDays`; `gold/day = E[profit]·unitsSoldPerDay` capped by estimated daily sales and by the player's share of the book. Rank opportunities by gold/day when capital is plentiful and by ROI/day when capital is the constraint (the community sources' "profit per hour" criterion).
- **Opportunity cost.** Benchmark = the ROI/day of the player's best *repeatable* alternative (computed from the ledger); a flip is worthwhile only if its ROI/day exceeds it. Inventory aging: the ledger gives days held per lot; apply the reservation-price idea qualitatively, i.e. the acceptable sell price should fall as held quantity, volatility and holding time rise (r = s − q·γ·σ²·τ), which justifies automatic markdown suggestions for stale stock.
- **Undercut cycles as a signal.** A sawtooth in `minPrice` (many small steps down, then one jump up) with stable `totalQty` indicates an Edgeworth-type undercut cycle: buy near the bottom of the tooth, post just after a reset. Detect via the ratio of count of small negative hourly changes to large positive jumps.
- **Failure modes.** p is not constant (competition camps the item); deposits for high-vendor-value items make 48 h repostings expensive; fees differ by game version (the cited figures are retail and community-sourced); the ledger is small for most items, so pool by item class; survivorship bias if only sold lots are analysed (include expiries).

### Gaps
- GW2 fee percentages (commonly cited as a 5% listing fee plus a 10% exchange fee) were seen only in search snippets; the GW2 wiki and ArenaNet support pages returned 403, so the numbers are unverified.
- No peer-reviewed estimate of sale probability or time-to-sale for MMO auction listings was found.
- The published *Quantitative Finance* (2008) version of Avellaneda & Stoikov and the full text of Ho & Stoll (1981) were not opened.
- Auction cut and deposit rules for the Forever/Classic-style client were not verified from an official source.

## 6. Position sizing and risk (Kelly, diversification, exposure caps relative to daily volume)

### Takeaway
The Kelly criterion gives the growth-optimal fraction of bankroll per bet from win probability and payoff, but it assumes known probabilities; with noisy estimates the standard practice is fractional Kelly. Independent of Kelly, exposure per item should be capped by what the market can absorb, which every reviewed game enforces or recommends in some form.

### Cited Findings
- **Original result.** Kelly (1956): define the exponential growth rate G = lim (1/N)·log(V_N/V_0). Betting everything each time maximizes expected capital but leads to ruin "with probability one" if continued indefinitely. Betting a fixed fraction ℓ on an even-money bet won with probability q (lost with p = 1−q) gives G = q·log(1+ℓ) + p·log(1−ℓ), maximized at 1+ℓ = 2q, i.e. ℓ = q − p; the gambler using this fraction "will, with probability one, eventually get ahead and stay ahead of one using any other ℓ". — [J. L. Kelly Jr., "A New Interpretation of Information Rate", *Bell System Technical Journal* 35 (July 1956), pp. 917–926 (reprint)](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/kelly_56.pdf) [peer-reviewed; full text read]
- **General payoff form and practical caveats.** f = p/l − q/g, where p = win probability, q = 1−p, g = fraction gained on a win, l = fraction lost on a loss (reduces to f* = p − q/b when the whole stake is lost). The criterion is valid only when probabilities are known, which the article says is almost never true for investments; overestimating the win probability raises the risk of ruin; practitioners bet half or quarter Kelly to reduce volatility and allow for model error at the cost of slower growth; Samuelson's objection is that log-growth ignores individual risk tolerance. — [Wikipedia, "Kelly criterion"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion) [reference] (the general formula and the fractional-Kelly guidance rest on a reference source only)
- **Arbitrage is capital-limited and risky.** Shleifer & Vishny (NBER WP 5167; *J. Finance* 52(1):35–55, 1997): real arbitrage is done by a few specialized, capital-constrained traders and can become "ineffective in extreme circumstances, when prices diverge far from fundamental values". — [NBER w5167](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5167) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Validated range of the impact law is small orders relative to daily volume.** Q/V from a few 10⁻⁴ to a few percent. — [Tóth et al. (2011), PRX](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694) [peer-reviewed]
- **Game-enforced and community exposure limits.** OSRS limits purchases per item per 4 hours, introduced by Jagex "to prevent price manipulation". — [OSRS Wiki, "Flipping"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Flipping) [community]. "Do not put all your money on one item"; four to six items suggested. — same page [community]. Trade many items to reduce the risk of a price crash on any single one, and never invest more than you can afford to lose (paraphrased). — [EVE University Wiki, "Trading"](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Trading) [community]. Don't invest your whole bank; bubbles at historic highs are the most dangerous time to buy. — [GE Tracker](https://www.ge-tracker.com/landing/osrs-flipping) [community]

### Inferences
- **Kelly for a flip.** Win = sells at target: g = (S(1−c) − B − expected lost deposits)/B. Loss = dump at fallback F: l = (B − F(1−c))/B. With p from the ledger: `f* = p/l − (1−p)/g`. Use ¼–½ of f* because p and g are estimated from small samples; clamp to [0, cap]. Display it as "suggested max gold in this item", not as an instruction.
- **Liquidity cap (more binding in practice).** `maxUnits = m·soldEstPerDay·targetHoldDays`, where m is the share of daily sales the player can realistically capture (their historical share of listings, or a conservative 10–25%). Position = min(Kelly amount, liquidity cap, per-item % of net worth).
- **Diversification.** Kelly as stated is for one bet at a time; flips on items in the same category (e.g. all raid consumables) are correlated through the same demand shock, so cap exposure per *category* as well as per item. Simple check from data: correlation of daily ROC between items in the player's inventory.
- **Decision supported.** How many units to buy when a deal is detected; when to stop adding to an existing position.
- **Failure modes.** p overestimated from a few lucky sales (ruin risk rises fast above full Kelly); payoff is not binary (partial sales, price drift while holding); bankroll that includes illiquid inventory overstates capacity; a market reset by a competitor can strand the whole position.

### Gaps
- Peer-reviewed treatment of fractional Kelly (MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba 2010, *Quantitative Finance*) returned 403 and was not verified; fractional-Kelly guidance here rests on Wikipedia.
- Markowitz (1952), "Portfolio Selection", was not opened; no finding is attributed to it.
- No study of optimal position sizing in virtual-item trading was found.

## 7. Arbitrage types: spatial, temporal, conversion/triangular; conversion chains as graph search

### Takeaway
Conversion chains (crafting, milling, disenchanting, essence up/down-conversion) map directly onto textbook graph problems: acyclic recipe trees are a longest-path problem on a DAG solvable in linear time, and reversible conversions are the currency-arbitrage problem solved by negative-cycle detection on −log rates. Peer-reviewed WoW evidence shows such conversion parities mostly hold (0% deviation at modal prices, about 5% at mean prices) and that cross-server price gaps stay inside a transaction-cost band and revert.

### Cited Findings
- **Arbitrage as negative-cycle detection.** "An arbitrage opportunity is a directed cycle such that the product of the exchange rates is greater than one"; replace each rate by the negated logarithm so products become sums and a profitable cycle becomes a negative-weight cycle; Bellman–Ford finds a shortest-paths tree or a reachable negative cycle in time proportional to E·V. — [Sedgewick & Wayne, *Algorithms* 4th ed., §4.4 Shortest Paths](https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/44sp/) [textbook]
- **Acyclic conversion graphs.** For an edge-weighted DAG, relaxing vertices in topological order solves shortest paths in time proportional to E + V and handles negative weights; longest paths by negating/reversing the relaxation; this is the basis of critical-path scheduling. Dijkstra requires non-negative weights. — [Sedgewick & Wayne §4.4](https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/44sp/) [textbook]
- **Conversion parity tested in WoW (peer-reviewed).** Three Lesser Planar Essences convert to one Greater and back. Game-wide auction data (allakhazam.com): modal prices were exactly 5.00 and 15.00 gold (0% deviation); mean prices were 6.02 and 17.12 versus parity-implied 5.71 and 18.06 (about −5.15% / +5.49%), i.e. "roughly 5% profit" from breaking Greater into Lesser, explained by level-70 players flooding the market with Greater essences without bothering to convert. — [Kosminsky (2010), JVWR 2(4)](https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/869/634) [peer-reviewed; full text read]
- **Spatial arbitrage and the law of one price across WoW servers (peer-reviewed).** Morrison & Fontenla (2013), *Empirical Economics* 44(3):1053–1064, DOI 10.1007/s00181-012-0567-3: data set of "eight structurally identical copies, or worlds, of a virtual macroeconomy"; the Phillips–Sul non-linear convergence test and the authors' own band-of-inaction test both find price convergence in all eight worlds. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/empeco/v44y2013i3p1053-1064.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]. Follow-up: price levels stay within a band of inaction created by trade barriers; divergences create arbitrage opportunities and quickly revert. — [Morrison & Fontenla (2017), *Economics Letters* 158:62–66](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecolet:v:158:y:2017:i:c:p:62-66) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]. The underlying dissertation modifies a PPP model to estimate how transportation costs affect PPP equilibrium. — [Morrison (2013), PhD dissertation, University of New Mexico](https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/econ_etds/11) [working paper/thesis; abstract only]
- **Where spatial arbitrage exists by design.** WoW retail: since patch 9.2.7 commodity listings are region-wide, while non-commodities such as armor and weapons remain realm-specific. — [Warcraft Wiki, "Auction House"](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Auction_House) [community]. EVE Online: each region has its own market; buying at one station, hauling and selling at another is a standard profession. — [EVE University Wiki, "Trading"](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Trading) [community]
- **Conversion values in existing tooling.** TSM exposes `Crafting` (sum of material prices of a recipe), `matPrice`, and `Destroy` (average value obtained from milling, prospecting or disenchanting, by default valued at DBMarket of the results). — [TSM documentation, price sources](https://support.tradeskillmaster.com/custom-strings/which-price-sources-can-i-use-and-what-do-they-mean) [community, tool documentation]
- **Limits of arbitrage.** Arbitrage needs capital and bears risk, and is weakest when mispricing is largest. — [Shleifer & Vishny, NBER w5167 / *J. Finance* 1997](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5167) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Temporal arbitrage (speculation) as practised.** Buying goods expecting a later price rise (e.g. ahead of predictable demand) requires a good understanding of the wider economy, per the page. — [EVE University Wiki, "Trading"](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Trading) [community]

### Inferences
- **Graph model.** Nodes = items (plus a "gold" node). Edges: *buy* gold→item at ask price; *sell* item→gold at `price·(1−c)` minus expected deposit; *convert* item(s)→item(s) with yield y (crafting output per input, expected mill/prospect/disenchant yield, vendor purchase). Edge weight = −ln(effective rate). A cycle gold→…→gold with negative total weight is a profitable chain; its profit factor = exp(−Σ weights).
- **Algorithm choice.** Pure crafting trees are DAGs: process in topological order and keep, per item, `bestCost = min(buyPrice, Σ inputs' bestCost/yield + craft fee)` and `bestValue = max(sellNet, Σ outputs' bestValue·yield)`; this is O(E+V) and is what a crafting-cost engine already does. Add Bellman–Ford only for reversible conversions (essence/shard/crystal style up- and down-conversion, vendor loops), limiting cycle length to keep it cheap.
- **Parity signal.** For any fixed conversion a:b, monitor `ratio = price_A·a / price_B·b`; flag when |ratio − 1| exceeds round-trip costs (cut + deposits + effort). Kosminsky's result suggests typical gaps around 5% at the mean and none at the mode, so thresholds must be net of fees.
- **Band of inaction.** No-trade band = [−cost, +cost] around parity where cost includes the 5% cut on the sell leg; only deviations beyond the band are actionable. The same framing applies to cross-faction, cross-realm or cross-city gaps where transfer is possible.
- **Temporal arbitrage** = buy at seasonal/regime lows and hold; computable as expected seasonal uplift (section 3 indices) minus holding costs and risk (section 5).
- **Failure modes.** Rates are quantity-dependent (each leg walks its ladder; the cheapest unit's price overstates the chain's profit for N units); multi-input recipes are hyperedges, not simple edges (a path search must cost *all* inputs); yields are random (use expected value, note variance); stale snapshot prices; cooldown- or skill-gated conversions; the output market may be too thin to absorb the result (apply the liquidity cap from section 6).

### Gaps
- The Morrison & Fontenla papers were verified at abstract level only; the size of the band of inaction, the goods basket and the sampling period were not readable (paywall; the open copy returned 403).
- Valve's economics blog post on arbitrage in the Team Fortress 2 barter economy (Varoufakis) could not be opened (TLS error) and is not cited.
- Albion Online's per-city markets and fee schedule could not be verified (wiki 403).

## 8. Supply shocks, scarcity signals, and cornering/"resetting" a market

### Takeaway
Removing supply raises price (causally identified in OSRS), and supply-side changes by the developer can move whole price indices by double digits within a quarter (EVE). Cornering works in theory and history but raises volatility and is hazardous; in MMO goods with near-zero entry barriers, new supply arrives quickly, so a reset is a bet on rivals' and farmers' response time.

### Cited Findings
- **Causal effect of supply removal.** OSRS item sink: +7% (round 1) and +14.4% (round 2) price for targeted high-value items with no significant change in trading volume; a 1% transaction tax had no detectable effect on volume at the 100 GP threshold, and an imprecise −6.9% (95% bounds −13% to −1%) at the 500M GP kink. — [Hogan-Hennessy, Xenopoulos & Silva (2022), arXiv:2210.07970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper]
- **Supply-side technology shock.** EVE Q1 2010: Tech II construction components fell 22% in the quarter; "the cause of this deflation seems to be entirely on the supply side" (moon material prices fell while Tech II ship construction grew). — [CCP QEN Q1 2010](https://cdn1.eveonline.com/community/QEN/QEN_Q1-2010.pdf) [official]
- **Demand concentration raises price; entry is cheap.** Across six WoW servers, the server in each population pair with a higher share of level 58–70 players had the higher netherweave cloth price (quadratic fit R² = 0.993 on six points). The paper argues barriers to entry are minimal because most goods are obtained by killing abundant monsters, so "more sellers will quickly enter" a profitable market. Low-population servers had less stable prices than high-population servers (average SD 1.62 vs 0.895), although medium-population servers were the least stable (2.35), contradicting a simple monotone relationship. — [Kosminsky (2010), JVWR 2(4)](https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/869/634) [peer-reviewed; full text read; small sample: one item, six servers, 30 days]
- **Trade-based manipulation can be profitable in theory.** Allen & Gale (1992), *Review of Financial Studies* 5(3):503–529: an uninformed speculator who "simply buys and sells shares" can profit in a rational-expectations model when investors attach positive probability to the manipulator being informed. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v5y1992i3p503-29.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Historical corners.** Allen, Litov & Mei (2006), *Review of Finance* 10(4):645–693: hand-collected stock and commodity corners 1863–1980; large investors and insiders have market power to manipulate prices; manipulation leading to a corner tends to increase market volatility and has adverse price impact on other assets; the presence of large investors makes trading against the mispricing risky; corners cause severe price distortions. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v10y2006i4p645-693.html) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Developer countermeasures.** OSRS buy limits exist "to prevent price manipulation"; Jagex can set guide prices manually if it detects manipulation or scams. — [OSRS Wiki, "Flipping"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Flipping) and ["Grand Exchange"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Grand_Exchange) [community]
- **Thin markets and bubbles.** Low-volume items are easily moved by single traders; hype can reverse quickly (Ranger gloves spike example); prices at historic highs may indicate a bubble. — [GE Tracker](https://www.ge-tracker.com/landing/osrs-flipping) [community]
- **Price restorations occur endogenously in undercutting markets.** Edgeworth cycles: a sharp spike followed by many small declines; less likely in both the least and the most concentrated markets. — [Doyle, Muehlegger & Samphantharak (2010)](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/edgeworth-cycles-revisited-0) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]
- **Aggregate outcomes are largely set by game design.** A new EverQuest II server launched during the study period "quickly" developed macroeconomic aggregates nearly identical to existing servers, which the authors read as "Code is Law". — [Castronova, Williams, Shen, Ratan, Xiong, Huang & Keegan (2009), *New Media & Society* 11(5):685–707, DOI 10.1177/1461444809105346](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444809105346) [peer-reviewed; abstract only, read via Crossref record]

### Inferences
- **Shortage signal from stored fields.** `ΔQty_k = totalQty_now / median(totalQty, last k days) − 1` and `ROC_k(minPrice)`. Shortage = quantity down sharply (e.g. below the 10th percentile of its own history) *and* price up; glut = quantity up and price down. Combine with `daysOfSupply` (section 4): a drop below ~1 day of estimated sales is the actionable scarcity threshold. `numListings` adds seller-concentration information: quantity steady but listings collapsing means a few sellers now own the book.
- **Reset detection.** Signature in snapshots: `totalQty` falls by a large fraction within one or two hours, then `minPrice` jumps and `numListings` drops while `avgStack = totalQty/numListings` rises (one owner reposting). Distinguish from organic sell-out by the reappearance of quantity at a higher price within hours.
- **Should the player reset? (economics).** Cost = `costToClear(P*)` from the ladder (not computable from the four fields). Revenue requires selling the acquired quantity at the new price before (a) undercutters and (b) fresh farmed/crafted supply arrive. Break-even resale fraction ≈ `costToClear / (unitsBought · P_new · (1−c))`. Refuse the idea when crafting or farming cost of the item is below the reset price (elastic supply caps the price at replacement cost plus effort) or when days of supply after the reset would exceed a few days.
- **Decision supported.** Stock up when a genuine shortage starts and the item has slow supply response (rare drops, cooldown crafts); avoid buying into someone else's reset near historic highs; sell inventory into a reset.
- **Failure modes.** Quantity drops caused by expiry waves or by one large seller logging off look like shortages; price jumps from a single high listing after a sell-out are not tradeable levels; region-wide commodity markets (retail) are far deeper than a realm snapshot suggests; the finance literature on corners concerns markets with short sellers and fixed supply, whereas MMO supply is produced on demand.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of market "resets" or cornering inside an MMO auction house was found; the manipulation evidence is from securities/commodities.
- Jarrow (1992), "Market manipulation, bubbles, corners, and short squeezes" (JFQA) was not opened.
- The speed of the supply response in WoW (how fast farmers and crafters refill a reset market) has no quantitative source; Kosminsky argues it qualitatively.

## 9. Elasticity of demand: necessities (consumables) versus luxuries (mounts, transmog)

### Takeaway
Textbook elasticity logic transfers: necessities with no close substitutes are price-inelastic (price rises barely reduce quantity, so shortages spike prices and raising price raises revenue), luxuries with substitutes are elastic. The one controlled virtual-world experiment found a basic consumable to be inelastic (−0.43).

### Cited Findings
- **Definition and formulas.** Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price (reported in absolute value). Midpoint method: %ΔQ = (Q₂−Q₁)/[(Q₂+Q₁)/2]; %ΔP = (P₂−P₁)/[(P₂+P₁)/2]. Elastic > 1, unitary = 1, inelastic < 1. — [OpenStax, *Principles of Economics 3e* §5.1 (Greenlaw, Shapiro & MacDonald, 2022)](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-1-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-price-elasticity-of-supply) [textbook]
- **Determinants.** Necessities with no close substitutes (life-saving drugs, gasoline) tend to have highly inelastic demand; luxury goods, items taking a large share of income and goods with many substitutes tend to have highly elastic demand. Goods with limited inputs (diamond rings, prime-location housing) have inelastic *supply*; goods whose inputs are readily available have elastic supply. — [OpenStax §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-2-polar-cases-of-elasticity-and-constant-elasticity) [textbook]
- **Revenue implication and time horizon.** With inelastic demand a price rise increases total revenue; with elastic demand a price cut increases it. Elasticities are often lower in the short run than the long run; in most markets prices move more than quantities in the short run. Sample values: housing 0.12, electricity 0.20, gasoline 0.35, restaurant meals 2.27. — [OpenStax §5.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/5-3-elasticity-and-pricing) [textbook]
- **Controlled experiment in a virtual world.** Castronova (2008): two identical copies of the game world "Arden", players randomly assigned (N = 43, about a month); the only difference was a health potion priced twice as high in the treatment world; players there bought 43.1% fewer potions, an implied demand elasticity of −0.431, which the author describes as well within the range expected for normal economic agents. — [Castronova, "A Test of the Law of Demand in a Virtual World", CESifo Working Paper 2355](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_2355.html) [working paper; abstract only]
- **Luxury-item evidence.** Removing supply of high-value OSRS items raised their price 7–14% while traded volume did not significantly change. — [Hogan-Hennessy et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper]
- **Demand shifts with the population that needs the good.** Netherweave cloth cost more on servers with a higher share of players in the level range that uses it. — [Kosminsky (2010)](https://jvwr-ojs-utexas.tdl.org/jvwr/article/view/869/634) [peer-reviewed]

### Inferences
- **Classification prior (no data needed).** Tag items by role: raid/dungeon consumables and profession materials = necessity-like (inelastic in the short run, strong weekly demand rhythm); mounts, pets, transmog = luxury (elastic, purchase is deferrable, price anchors matter); crafted gear = in between, with substitutes from drops.
- **Empirical elasticity from the addon's data.** Arc elasticity between two periods: `E = [(S₂−S₁)/((S₂+S₁)/2)] / [(P₂−P₁)/((P₂+P₁)/2)]`, with S = estimated units sold per day (section 4) and P = daily robust price. **Identification caveat:** observed (P, S) pairs mix demand shifts and supply shifts; only periods where the price change was plausibly supply-driven (shortage or glut detected in section 8 while the day-of-week is held constant) trace out the demand curve. With only listing snapshots, S is itself an estimate, so E is at best a coarse inelastic/elastic label. For items the player sells, the ledger gives a cleaner version: own units sold per day at different own prices.
- **Decisions supported.** For inelastic items, hold out for price during shortages and avoid deep undercuts (a lower price does not create more buyers, it only transfers margin); for elastic items, expect volume only at competitive prices and expect long holding times at high prices; size inventory of luxuries small (section 6).
- **Failure modes.** Castronova's estimate is one item, 43 players, a forced 2× price difference; WoW consumable demand is also tied to content cadence (a raid tier ending collapses demand regardless of price); substitutes change with patches.

### Gaps
- No empirical elasticity estimates from real WoW, EVE, OSRS or GW2 market data were found.
- Income elasticity (how demand for luxuries scales with player wealth/inflation) was not researched.

## 10. Published research and data analyses on MMO economies, and what trading tools in other games show

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed empirical work on MMO market data is thin but consistent: virtual markets behave like competitive real markets (law of demand, law of one price, design-determined aggregates), with larger fluctuations. Most quantitative signal design lives in developer reports (EVE) and community tools (TSM, OSRS wiki/GE Tracker, GW2 trackers), which converge on the same core fields: a robust price, buy/sell spread or margin net of fees, traded volume, supply/demand counts and limits.

### Cited Findings
**Empirical papers using WoW auction data**
- Kosminsky (2010), "World of Warcraft: The Viability of MMORPGs as Platforms for Modeling and Evaluating Perfect Competition", *J. Virtual Worlds Research* 2(4). **Measured:** 30-day price mean/median/SD and volume of netherweave cloth; share of level 58–70 players; price parity of Lesser vs Greater Planar Essence. **Sample:** six servers (two each low/medium/high population), 13 Sep–13 Oct 2008, ≥ 1,000 auctions each (wowecon.com); census from WarcraftRealms; game-wide essence prices from Allakhazam. **Result:** high-population servers had more stable prices than low (SD 0.895 vs 1.62; F = 3.76, p < .001) but medium were least stable (2.35); price rose with the share of relevant-level players (R² = .993, six points); conversion parity exact at modal prices, ~5% off at means. Concludes WoW is "a highly competitive market approaching perfect competition". ### Inferences
- **Convergent signal set across games** (what a WoW addon should show to be on par): robust market value with short and long windows (TSM); margin/profit net of fees and ROI (OSRS, GW2, EVE tools); traded volume or sale rate (OSRS API hourly counts; must be estimated in WoW); supply and, where a bid side exists, demand counts (GW2); liquidity filters before ranking (gw2tp presets); per-item position limits (OSRS buy limits, emulated as a liquidity cap).
- **What is structurally different in WoW:** no buy orders (no observable demand side or instant-sell price), no published trade volume, deposits forfeited on failed listings, and hourly snapshot granularity. Consequently "margin" must be defined against the player's own acquisition cost (bought, crafted or converted) rather than a bid–ask spread.
- **Research supports treating a WoW realm market as competitive:** prices respond to demand composition, conversion parities hold within fees, cross-market gaps revert. Edges are therefore small and fee-bounded for liquid commodities; larger edges exist only where liquidity is thin (and risk is higher).

## 11. Inflation: faucets and sinks, erosion of raw-gold farming, and holding goods versus gold

### Takeaway
Developers measure virtual inflation with basket price indices and track currency faucets and sinks explicitly; the evidence shows inflation is neither uniform nor guaranteed (OSRS indices ranged from −5% to +12% over six months by price tier; EVE saw broad deflation in a quarter because production got cheaper). The implication for a trader is item-specific: goods whose supply is constrained hold value against currency inflation, goods whose production gets easier lose value even when gold inflates.

### Cited Findings
- **Faucets, sinks and index construction in a live economy.** CCP publishes monthly sinks-and-faucets charts, money supply, money velocity and four price indices; total ISK faucets rose 17% in March 2026. — [CCP MER March 2026](https://www.eveonline.com/news/view/monthly-economic-report-march-2026) [official]. Index method: Laspeyres indices with the basket base updated monthly from the previous month's traded totals. — [CCP QEN Q1 2010](https://cdn1.eveonline.com/community/QEN/QEN_Q1-2010.pdf) [official]
- **Deflation from supply-side change.** EVE Q1 2010: CPI −2.1%, PPPI −14.2% in one quarter, caused by more efficient production introduced in an expansion. — [CCP QEN Q1 2010](https://cdn1.eveonline.com/community/QEN/QEN_Q1-2010.pdf) [official]
- **Heterogeneous inflation by price tier.** OSRS, six months to Dec 2018: lower-quartile index −0.18%, lower-mid +4.91%, upper-mid +11.97%, upper −5.30%, volume-weighted top-100 +9.81%; the authors judge the roughly 12% as not hyperinflation but worth monitoring, and define inflation as index change between start and end as a percentage. — [Scholten et al. (2019), arXiv:1905.06721](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06721) [working paper]
- **Transaction tax as a currency sink.** OSRS Grand Exchange tax (2%, capped at 5M per item): most of the collected coins are removed from the game, a smaller part buys specific items from players and deletes them (item sink); a Jagex moderator stated the item sink spends less than the tax collects. — [OSRS Wiki, "Grand Exchange"](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Grand_Exchange) [community] (rests on a community source). Effects of that tax and sink on volume and prices: — [Hogan-Hennessy et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper]
- **WoW's auction cut and forfeited deposits are sinks of the same type** (5% cut; deposits lost on unsold or cancelled auctions). — [Warcraft Wiki, "Auction House"](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Auction_House) [community] (rests on a community source)
- **Auction houses as anti-inflation design.** A market is described as "basically the best way to sink (remove) virtual items or currency from the players to avoid economic inflation", which is "always a threat since a MMOG is persistent". — [Hsu, Togelius & Sun (2014)](https://www.cse.unr.edu/~sushil/pubs/newestPapers/aux/paper_104.pdf) [peer-reviewed conference short paper; statement is from the introduction, not a result]
- **Design framework (secondary summary of the standard textbook).** A reader's summary of Lehdonvirta & Castronova (2014) reports: faucets create assets, sinks remove them (transaction taxes, repair/travel fees, decaying items), transformations such as crafting act as both; inflation occurs when faucet inflow exceeds sink outflow; deflation causes hoarding while mild inflation encourages spending; designers should track money supply per capita and faucet-versus-sink flows; when inflation is high, add attractive expensive sinks (cosmetics, status mounts) rather than cutting player income. — [A. Buteau, book summary of *Virtual Economies*](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/virtual-economies-book-summary/) [community] (secondary summary; the book itself was not opened, so treat as unverified against the original)
- **Real-money value of game currency.** EverQuest's currency traded at about USD 0.0107 per unit in 2001. — [Castronova (2001), CESifo WP 618](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_618.html) [working paper; abstract only]. OSRS gold on illicit markets sold at about USD 0.45–0.58 per million GP in 2021–22, with no structural break during the tax/sink interventions. — [Hogan-Hennessy et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07970) [working paper]
- **Aggregates follow design.** — [Castronova et al. (2009), DOI 10.1177/1461444809105346](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444809105346) [peer-reviewed; abstract only]

### Inferences
- **Why raw-gold farming erodes.** A raw-gold activity pays a fixed nominal amount per hour set by game code, while the prices of player-traded goods float. If currency faucets outpace sinks, the price index rises and the same gold buys fewer goods; activities that produce *goods* are indexed to the price level automatically. This follows from the faucet/sink framework and the index evidence above; no source measured it directly for WoW.
- **Addon price index (computable).** Laspeyres-style: pick a basket of the N most liquid items with weights w_i = estimated units sold × price in the base period; `Index_t = Σ w_i·(p_i,t/p_i,0) / Σ w_i`, using daily robust prices; rebase monthly as CCP does. Report sub-indices by category (materials, consumables, luxuries), since the OSRS data show tiers moving in opposite directions. Use the index to (a) deflate price histories before computing z-scores over long windows, (b) express the ledger's profit in "real" terms, (c) set the hurdle for holding inventory versus gold.
- **Goods versus gold.** Holding goods beats holding gold only for items whose own supply faucet is not expanding: compare each item's price trend with the index (relative strength = item ROC − index ROC). Items with persistently negative relative strength (being made obsolete or easier to produce) lose value even under inflation; EVE's 2010 deflation is the cautionary case.
- **Personal faucet/sink ledger.** From the ledger, total auction cut paid plus deposits forfeited is the player's contribution to the sink; showing it per item makes relisting costs visible.
- **Failure modes.** A basket of auction prices measures only player-traded goods (not vendor prices or services); weights rest on estimated sales; expansions reset the relevant basket; realm-level indices are noisy for small populations.

### Gaps
- No published, verified measurement of inflation in World of Warcraft (retail or Classic) was found; a thesis reporting that WoW prices rise as servers age could not be opened.
- Lehdonvirta & Castronova (2014) was not accessible; the framework statements rely on a community summary and on consistent usage in the official EVE and OSRS sources.
- The World Bank/infoDev report "Knowledge Map of the Virtual Economy" (Lehdonvirta & Ernkvist, 2011) on gold farming had no readable abstract on the pages opened; no figures are cited from it.
- CCP's reports do not define money velocity or the index formulas on the March 2026 page itself; the Laspeyres definition comes from the 2010 newsletter and may have changed.
- Whether gold or goods held value better over any specific WoW period was not measurable from the sources found.
